CVFR Nav — Privacy Policy

Effective: 28 November 2026

CVFR Nav is a moving-map assistant for VFR pilots in Israel, the United States and Romania. This policy covers CVFR Nav on the App Store, the CVFR Nav web planner, and the CVFR Nav Video program for Windows.

What the app collects

The developer receives no data from the App Store version of CVFR Nav, except what you choose to publish with Live sharing: no account, no analytics, no advertising, no tracking. (The developer's own services are hosted by Vercel, which keeps short-lived request logs — see Services the app contacts.) The one thing that leaves the device on its own is an approximate position for the weather forecast, sent to Open-Meteo and not linked to you (see Weather below) — and, only while the app is in its United States region, the same kind of approximate position (rounded to about a kilometre) to NOAA's Aviation Weather Center for the weather reports round you, and a coarser one (the half-degree square of the map you are in, about 30 NM across) to the developer's own service for the NOTAMs round you; neither is linked to you or kept (see The US region below).

When you choose to share a flight live, what the link shows is published as described in Live sharing below — only then, and only for that flight. The web planner sends anonymous usage statistics per visit (see The web planner).

What stays on your device

  • Your position, from the device's location services, is used on the device to draw the map and raise alerts. It is not sent to the developer — except, in the United States region only, as the half-degree square of the map you are in (about 30 NM across), to ask for the NOTAMs round you (see The US region).
  • Recorded flights (track, altitude, the device's motion and barometer readings, events, a line about the device's health once a minute — the app's processor use, battery level, how warm the device is, whether Low Power Mode is on — and what the map showed during the flight: the active flight plan's route and aircraft — callsign, type, persons on board, not your filed plan's text with your name, licence and phone, and not the weight-and-balance load (the weights of pilot and passengers), which stays with the saved plan — the NOTAMs in force, the radio reports and the aerodrome weather reports) and, if you switch it on, cockpit audio are saved in the app's own storage on your device. They leave it when you share or export a flight yourself, to the destination you choose, and — with Sync flights and plans with iCloud on (Settings → Data and about; on unless you switch it off) — in your own iCloud Drive, so your other devices signed in to the same Apple Account have the same flights — and the flight videos you make, which leave the device a week after they reached iCloud and stay there until you delete them. That copy is stored by Apple under your account (Apple's privacy policy); the developer cannot read it. It is also shown in the Files app (iCloud Drive → CVFR Nav). Each flight goes with its signature, made with a key that never leaves this device, and that key's public half — the same for every flight from the device — so your other devices can tell when a flight was changed after it was signed. Deleting a flight in the app deletes it on all your devices. Beside each flight the app keeps its approach debrief (<flight>.approaches.json: each approach's runway, heights, speeds and track as the flight page judges them, worked out from the flight itself) — on the device and, with that sync on, in the same iCloud folder, for your own video tool on your PC; it goes and is deleted with its flight, and holds nothing the flight does not. A debrief recording — the conversation after a flight — is made only when you tap Record debrief on a flight's page; a red bar shows on every screen while it records. It uses your device's own microphone and may hold the voices of the people with you: tell them. It is kept with the flight on your devices and, with iCloud sync on, in your own iCloud Drive; it is never sent to us — not with the test builds' flight uploads, not with a live share. Delete it any time ("Delete debrief" on the flight's page, which removes it from your other devices too); it is deleted with its flight. It leaves your devices only when you share it yourself. Each flight file also names the device it was recorded on: the fingerprint of that device's signing key (a short code, the same on every flight from the device), the exact device model and the system version. It travels with the flight wherever the flight goes — a flight you share or export, the iCloud sync and a test build's upload (below). CVFR Nav assumes your Apple Account is yours alone and that every device signed in to it is your own: an account or a device shared between several people (a club iPad, say) shares their flights and cockpit audio too, at the account holder's own choice and responsibility — switch sync off on such a device.
  • Flight videos you make are kept on the device until you delete them (or, with sync on, a week after they reached iCloud, as above). Save to Photos adds a video to your photo library when you tap it; the app asks only to add photos. It reads your photo library only for a live share you start, with your permission, and never the photos' location (see Live sharing).
  • Saved plans and planning details — each plan (with its weight-and-balance load: the weights of pilot and passengers), your pilot details (name, licence, phone), each aircraft's record with its free-flight weight-and-balance load (the weights you entered for a flight without a plan) and the flight-planning settings — are kept in the app's storage and, with Sync flights and plans with iCloud on and the device signed in to iCloud with iCloud Drive on, in your own iCloud Drive (Files → iCloud Drive → CVFR Nav → Planning), so your other devices on the same Apple Account have the same plans (since 2026-10-18). Since 2026-11-26 the same planning file is also kept as a record in your own iCloud private database (CloudKit), so your devices — and the CVFR Nav web planner when you sign in there with your Apple Account (see The web planner) — can read it. Both copies are stored by Apple under your account; the developer cannot read them. Deleting a plan deletes it on all your devices.
  • The flight plan email: when you tap to send a plan, the app hands the email — the plan's ICAO text, a short note, and your name, licence number and phone from your pilot details — to your own default mail app as a link, addressed to the briefing office (ais@iaa.gov.il). Nothing is sent by the app itself, and nothing goes to the developer: you send it, or not, from your mail app, under that app's own terms.
  • Prior permission emails: for an Israeli aerodrome whose AIP chapter asks for an email before a visit (for example Ramon, LLER), the plan page prepares that email — the aircraft's registration and type, the date and times, the route, the persons on board, and your name and phone from your pilot details — and hands it to your own default mail app, addressed to the address printed in that aerodrome's AIP chapter. As with the flight plan email, the app sends nothing itself and nothing goes to the developer. What you mark ("Sent", "Not needed") is kept with the plan (and synced with it, as above).
  • Reminders: the reminders before a plan's filing or prior-permission deadline, and after the landing of a US plan you marked filed (to close it), are local notifications scheduled on your device by iOS (which asks your permission first); they are never sent from a server and nothing about them leaves the device.
  • Flight briefs (PDF) hold the plan with your name, licence and phone. The app keeps them only until you have shared or saved them; they are cleared at the next launch.
  • Weather: for the forecast, the app sends Open-Meteo (open-meteo.com) points rounded to about a kilometre — along a flight plan's route, the aircraft's position for the wind shown on the map (when it has moved 10 NM, or every 30 minutes), a spot you tap — with the hours wanted; nothing else about you. Open-Meteo keeps its request logs (which include those points and your internet address) for up to 90 days to run its service, and shares them with no one (its terms). The aerodrome reports come from the Israel Meteorological Service (ims.gov.il) and NOAA's Aviation Weather Center (aviationweather.gov) and need nothing from you. Nothing goes to the developer.
  • The barometer offset the app learns for your phone is kept on the device.
  • Photos of the sky for the cloud-cover estimate are analysed on the device and not stored or sent anywhere.
  • Your GoPro, if you pair one (Settings → Recording and audio → GoPro): the app talks to that camera over Bluetooth, and only to it — to set its date, time and time zone from your phone and to start and stop its recording with yours. iOS asks for Bluetooth only when you tap "Pair a GoPro". The app keeps the camera's name and the identifier iOS gives it on this phone; nothing about the camera or its footage is sent anywhere, and the app never reads the camera's media. What happened (clock set, recording started or stopped, the HiLight tags sent while recording) is written into the recorded flight. With auto record on, the app may start and stop the camera while it runs in the background — iOS's Bluetooth background mode, used only to reach the paired camera by its identifier, never to look for other devices.
  • Auto record (off unless you choose a headset in Settings → Recording and audio → Auto record): when that headset connects, the app starts an ordinary recording, and stops it a few minutes after it disconnects. To hear the headset connect, iOS must keep the app running, so while auto record is on the app keeps a coarse location (about 3 km, from cell towers and Wi-Fi, no GPS; iOS shows the location arrow) — in the background on the ground with nothing recording the GPS, the alerts and the weather requests stop. (In the air the app keeps running in the background with its GPS, so the alerts go on whether or not you record; on the ground it stops.) That coarse location is used only to keep the app running; it is not stored and not sent anywhere. To offer the headsets to choose from, the app remembers the headsets it has seen as an audio route (at most eight: the name iOS gives each and its Bluetooth device address, or a wired port's identifier) — only while auto record is on; they stay on the device and are never synced.
  • Settings other than the flight-planning ones stay on the device (the map, alerts, audio and recording settings are never synced); a recording keeps a copy of the plan it was flown with, as above.
  • The diagnostic log — a rolling log of what the app did (screens opened, flights saved and replayed, memory use, problems), kept with iOS's crash reports in the app's own storage on the device so a problem can be found afterwards. It holds no names, no audio and no NOTAM text; positions in it are rounded to about a kilometre. It keeps about two days and at most 1 MB, the oldest deleted first. It never leaves the device on its own: only when you share it yourself (Settings → Data and about → Share diagnostics), to the destination you choose.

Services the app contacts

  • NOTAMs for Israel and Romania — the app asks the developer's own service for the region's NOTAMs (Eurocontrol EAD's, obtained by the developer, for use in the app only). The request names only the region (Israel or Romania) and carries the app's attestation (App Attest or Play Integrity, as under "App Attest" below); nothing about you or your position.
  • Edition file — the app reads which edition of the CAA chart is currently published from cvfr.idra-tech.io/edition.json (GitHub Pages), to tell you when its data is out of date. The request carries nothing about you or your position.
  • Version file — at launch the app reads cvfr.idra-tech.io/version.json (GitHub Pages) to tell you when a newer version is available: the App Store's line for the App Store version, the TestFlight lines (internal or external testers) for a test build. The comparison is made on the device; the request carries nothing about you, your version or your position.
  • Radio reports — while the app is open or recording a flight, it asks once a minute for the runway, wind and QNH read from public air traffic radio, from the developer's isr-atc service (llhz-now.vercel.app and isr-atc.idra-tech.io, hosted by Vercel). The request names only the airfield or unit; it carries nothing about you or your position. Like any web request it reaches the host with your device's IP address; Vercel keeps short-lived request logs (IP address, time, path), which the developer does not use to identify anyone (Vercel's privacy policy).
  • Aerodrome weather — every 10 minutes while the app is open or recording, the app reads the aerodrome reports and forecasts from the Israel Meteorological Service (ims.gov.il) and NOAA's Aviation Weather Center (aviationweather.gov). The requests carry nothing about you or your position; like any web request they reach those hosts with your device's IP address.
  • CVFR Nav's weather service — every 10 minutes while the app is open or recording, the app asks the developer's own service (on Vercel) for the Israel Meteorological Service's station readings. The request carries a proof that it comes from the genuine app — Apple's App Attest: a key made on the device for this purpose alone, certified once by Apple's servers (the device asks Apple to vouch for the key; no personal data goes with it), which says nothing about you; the service checks it and keeps nothing (where App Attest is unavailable, the app's key) — and nothing about you or your position; like any web request it reaches the host with your device's IP address. The developer's hosting provider, Vercel, keeps short-lived request logs (IP address, time, path), which the developer does not use to identify anyone (Vercel's privacy policy). The same service gives Megiddo aerodrome's runway wind, read from the aerodrome operator's own wind station (with the operator's agreement): the app asks for it at most once a minute, only while Megiddo is in the plan on screen, tapped on the map, or within about 25 NM. That request names only the aerodrome and carries nothing about you or your position — the app's distance to Megiddo is worked out on the phone; the service, not your phone, contacts the operator's station.
  • The app's identification in web requests — every request above carries iOS's standard User-Agent header, which names the app, its build number and the iOS networking version (for example "CVFRNav/1193 CFNetwork/… Darwin/…"); nothing that identifies you or the device.
  • Apple Maps — the map background and the place name shown when you tap the map come from Apple's map services, under Apple's privacy policy.
  • Google Maps — CVFR Nav includes Google Maps features. When you choose one of the Google map styles for the moving map (Google terrain, Google hybrid, or in the US the sectional over Google), its background comes from Google Maps (Google's Maps SDK for iOS), and Google receives your device's IP address and the area of the map shown — which, while the map follows the aircraft, moves with the aircraft's position, so Google can tell roughly where you are. Google's SDK also declares (in its privacy manifest) that it collects a device identifier (linked to you, for analytics and the map's functioning) and, for analytics, how the map is used, its performance and other usage data, and crash data — all under the Google Maps/Google Earth Additional Terms of Service and Google's Privacy Policy; none of it tracks you across other companies' apps. The app turns the SDK's own crash reports to Google off. Every other map — the route builder, a flight's page, its video, the approach debrief, a shared flight's card — stays on Apple's map, and nothing of Google's map is saved or shared. With Apple's map styles nothing is sent to Google.
  • Israel's aerodrome charts — the aerodrome charts of Israel's Aeronautical Information Publication (approach, departure and arrival procedures, parking and the aerodrome charts) are downloaded directly from the Civil Aviation Authority's eAIP e-aip.azurefd.net (hosted on Microsoft's Azure) and kept on the device: those of the aerodromes in your plans and along them, of the aerodromes within about 50 NM of the aircraft, and of any aerodrome whose charts you open. Each request names only the chart's file (for example LL_AD_2_LLER_ADC_en.PDF) and carries nothing else about you — no position is sent — but which aerodromes are asked for follows your plans and roughly where the aircraft is. Like any web request it reaches the host with your device's IP address. Nothing is sent while the app is in its US region.

The US region

The app can be switched to its United States region (Settings → Region, or the offer the app shows when the device is in the US). The US region shows the FAA's data for display only: no alerts, and US flight plans are never filed by the app. Nothing below is sent while the app is in its Israel region. In the US region:

  • METARs for the altimeter setting — the app asks NOAA's Aviation Weather Center (aviationweather.gov, the US government's public data service) for the METARs of the stations round the aircraft: the request names a box of about 100 NM round the aircraft with its corners rounded to two decimals (about a kilometre), at most every 10 minutes or 25 NM, only while the app is on screen or recording.
  • An airport's METAR and TAF — when you open a US airport, the app asks aviationweather.gov for its reports by the airport's identifier (for example KFRG); the request names only the airport.
  • The NWS's model forecast (LAMP) — when you open a US airport or a US plan, the app downloads the National Weather Service's whole GFS LAMP bulletin from tgftp.nws.noaa.gov (one file for every US station, at most about once an hour); the request names no airport and no position.
  • NOTAMs — the app asks CVFR Nav's own service (on Vercel, as above) for the NOTAMs of the half-degree square of the map (about 30 NM across) holding the area round the aircraft — or, without a position, round the centre of the map — every 10 minutes or after 20 NM; and, for a US plan, of the squares along its route. The request names only the square's centre on that grid and a radius, so every user in the same square asks the same thing; nothing finer than the square leaves the device. The service asks the FAA's NOTAM service for them (the FAA's key stays on the service) and keeps nothing; the special-use airspace offices' NOTAMs are asked by office, with no position.
  • The shared ticket — the NOTAM, special-use airspace and TFR requests carry no App Attest proof of their own: they carry a shared ticket, a code the same for every user of the app and changed every half hour, which the app gets from the service with a request proved by App Attest (as the weather service's). Because these answers are public FAA data and the request carries nothing of the caller's, Vercel's network may answer it from a copy kept for everyone. Where no ticket can be had, the request carries the App Attest proof instead.

  • The FAA's sectional charts — with the Sectional map style, and to store a US plan's charts for use without internet (or the charts round you, when you ask), the app downloads the chart's pieces ("tiles") from the FAA's own chart service on ArcGIS Online (tiles.arcgis.com, run by Esri for the FAA), and reads which chart edition it holds from www.arcgis.com. A tile request names a square of the chart (its zoom level, row and column), never your position; the tiles asked for show the area you are looking at or planning to fly. The stored tiles stay on your device (not in its backup) and are deleted with the plan or from Settings.

  • TFRs — every 10 minutes the app asks CVFR Nav's own service for the FAA's list of temporary flight restrictions with their shapes (the whole country's, from the FAA's TFR site tfr.faa.gov); the request carries no position, only the shared ticket.

  • Obstacles — the FAA's Digital Obstacle File is part of the app; showing it sends nothing.

  • A US flight plan's text — the app writes a US plan's ICAO flight plan text (with your name, phone and aircraft) on the device for you to copy into 1800wxbrief.com or read to a briefer at 1-800-WX-BRIEF; nothing is sent by the app. Copied, it stays on this device's clipboard only (not your other devices') for 10 minutes. What you mark ("I filed this plan", "I closed it") is kept with the plan (and synced with it, as above). After the landing of a plan you marked filed, the app reminds you to close it — on the screen and as a local notification (see Reminders), which does not name the plan.

  • Airport diagrams — the FAA's airport diagrams are downloaded from the developer's own site, cvfr.idra-tech.io (GitHub Pages), and kept on the device: those of the airports in your US plans and of the airports within about 50 NM of the aircraft, each once per FAA cycle (28 days), with a small file naming the current cycle read at most every few hours. Each request names only the airport (for example KFRG.png) and carries nothing else about you; which airports are asked for follows your plans and roughly where the aircraft is. Like any web request it reaches GitHub with your device's IP address (GitHub's privacy statement).

  • Region data updates — for a region whose data comes as a downloadable pack (Romania), the app asks the developer's own service (the one that serves the weather, hosted by Vercel) for a small index of that region's data, at most once a day while you are on the ground with the app in that region, and downloads a newer AIRAC cycle when one is published. The requests are signed with Apple's App Attest as the service's other requests are; they name only the region and the cycle and carry nothing else about you; like any web request they reach Vercel with your device's IP address.
  • Introductory period — on Android, during the introductory period, the first time the app runs with a connection it asks the developer's own service (on Vercel) for a small signed note that the app joined then: the date, a random id and the period's name. If you have signed in to Google for the plans sync, the note also carries a one-way hash of your Google account's id — never your address, never the id itself — and a copy is kept in your Google Drive app-data folder beside your plans, so a new phone signed in to the same account keeps it. The request is signed with Google's Play Integrity as the service's other requests are and carries nothing else about you; the service stores nothing. The note stays on your device; it is used only to recognise, when paid plans arrive, that you joined during the introductory period. On iPhone and iPad nothing is sent for this: the App Store's own record of when you first got the app is used.
  • Airport charts — the FAA's other airport charts (approach, departure and arrival procedures, minimums, hot spots) and the airports' pages of the FAA's Chart Supplement are downloaded directly from the FAA's server aeronav.faa.gov and kept on the device: those of the airports in and along your US plans, of the airports within about 50 NM of the aircraft, and of any airport whose charts you open; the list of each cycle's charts and of each edition's Chart Supplement pages comes from the developer's site as above. Each request names only the chart's or the page's file (for example 00626IL11.PDF or ne_201_29OCT2026.pdf) and carries nothing else about you; like any web request it reaches the FAA with your device's IP address.

Nothing else about you is sent; like any web request these reach their hosts with your device's IP address, and Vercel keeps its short-lived request logs (IP address, time, path — which here includes the half-degree square of a NOTAM request) as above.

The Romania region

The app can be switched to its Romania region (Settings → Region). It shows the data of AIP Romania for display only: no alerts, and plans are never filed by the app. Nothing below is sent while the app is in its Israel region. In the Romania region:

  • METARs and TAFs — as in the US region (above): the app asks NOAA's Aviation Weather Center (aviationweather.gov) for the METARs of the stations round the aircraft, by a box with its corners rounded to two decimals, at most every 10 minutes or 25 NM, only while the app is on screen or recording; and, when you open an aerodrome, for its reports by the aerodrome's identifier (for example LROP). Nothing else about you is in these requests.
  • NOTAMs and region data updates — as under "NOTAMs for Israel and Romania" and "Region data updates" above: the developer's own service, the request naming only the region.
  • The forecast is asked as everywhere (Open-Meteo, above). The plan's text is written on the device for you to send; nothing is sent by the app.

Live sharing (only when you share)

Share live sends friends a link to follow one of your flights. Nothing is shared until you choose Start sharing (or Record and share live); sharing stops when you choose End sharing, after the landing, or at the end of the flight plan being flown. It needs you signed in to iCloud.

Anyone who has the link can see, without signing in:

  • The aircraft's positions — latitude and longitude, altitude, ground speed and track — and its trail, published late by the delay you choose (1, 2, 5 or 15 minutes; 2 by default, never live): nobody, not even the developer, can get the position of the moment from the app. The status (planned, on the ground, flying, landed, sharing ended) and events (take-off, landing, the plan's points passed) carry the same delay.
  • The share's title and route: the flight plan's aerodromes and points (for a plan shared before its flight, its planned departure time); the aircraft's callsign only if "Show callsign" is on (off by default).
  • Photos, only those you add, or — if you allow the app to read your photo library — those taken during the shared flight on your devices: each is re-encoded on the phone from its pixels alone, with no metadata (no location, camera or date fields), and placed on the track by the time it was taken; captions you write.
  • A preview picture of the flight for the link (drawn on the phone over OpenStreetMap and Natural Earth geography), and after the landing the flight page: the smoothed track, distance and duration.
  • Time on the ground away from an airfield (for example a drive between two fields) is shown as a drive, not a flight, and only 48 hours later; while not recording away from any airfield, only "on the ground, away from the airfield", with no position.

Never shared: your name (unless you type it into a title), licence, phone, the filed flight plan's text, weight and balance, persons on board, cockpit audio, sensor data, NOTAMs and alerts.

Hide near my home field (Settings → Live sharing, off by default): no position within 1 NM of the aerodrome you choose is sent, and the aerodrome is not named.

Where it is kept: Apple's CloudKit public database of the app's iCloud container, written under your iCloud account. A share's records can be read only by their link's name (128 random bits): they have no index, so they cannot be listed or searched. CloudKit adds to every record an identifier of your iCloud account that is specific to this app, and of the device — anyone who holds two of your links could tell that they come from the same person; the page never shows them. The developer, as the container's owner, can read the records; they are not used for anything else, analysed, sold or shared.

How long: the flight page stays until you delete its link, or for a day or a week if you choose so in Keep flight page. Delete link (asked first) deletes the share's records — the positions, the photos, the picture — and every copy of the link then shows "not shared". A share that stops sending without ending (for example the phone switched off) shows "Sharing stopped" 12 hours later. Your links are listed in Your shared flights on all your devices (through your own iCloud), where each can be deleted. If you remove the app before deleting a link, its records stay: install the app again, signed in to the same iCloud account, and delete the link from Your shared flights.

Deletion on request: any pilot, or anyone shown on a shared flight (in a photo, for example), may ask the developer to delete that shared flight: write to cvfr.support@idra-tech.io with its link (or say which flight it is). The developer deletes the share's records — the positions, the photos, the picture — within 30 days, and the link then shows "not shared". Copies others have made — a screenshot, a saved photo, a preview of the link kept by a messaging app — are outside the developer's reach.

The page: the link opens the developer's page (cvfr.idra-tech.io/live, GitHub Pages; the link preview at live.cvfr.idra-tech.io through the developer's service on Vercel, which keeps short-lived request logs). The map on it is Apple's MapKit JS, loaded in the viewer's browser. The page keeps the viewer's language, map type and panel choice in the browser's own storage; it sets no cookies and runs no analytics or advertising.

Free: live sharing costs nothing today; it may become a paid feature later, and this policy will say so first.

The web planner (only when you sign in)

The CVFR Nav web planner (cvfr-nav.idra-tech.io, when available) is a page for planning flights in Israel on a computer's browser, for pilots who use the CVFR Nav app: it runs the app's own planning rules in the browser and syncs plans with your devices. It has no NOTAMs, weather or weight-and-balance calculations. It shows only a sign-in screen until you sign in with your Apple Account or your Google account; one at a time, and the two never sync with each other.

  • The page is served by GitHub Pages (cvfr-nav.idra-tech.io); like any web request it reaches GitHub with your device's IP address (GitHub's privacy statement). It is kept out of search engines. Apple's sign-in script is loaded from Apple (cdn.apple-cloudkit.com) when the page opens, and Google's (accounts.google.com) only when you choose Sign in with Google; those requests reach Apple and Google with your IP address. After you sign in, the page downloads the planner's program and the aeronautical data from the same site, encrypted; those requests carry nothing about you.
  • Sign in with Apple — through Apple's CloudKit JS. The page then reads and writes, in your own iCloud private database of the CVFR Nav container, only: the planning records your devices keep there (one per device: your plans with their weight-and-balance loads, your pilot details — name, licence, phone —, your aircraft records and own aircraft types, and the flight-planning settings; see Saved plans and planning details above), its own record as one more of your devices, and the key grant your app saved there (below). They are stored by Apple under your account; the developer cannot read them. If you let Apple share your name, the page shows it beside "Apple" in its toolbar; it is not stored. Apple's CloudKit JS keeps you signed in on this browser (its session, kept in the browser by Apple's script) until you sign out with Apple's Sign out button.
  • Sign in with Google — through Google's sign-in (Google Identity Services). CVFR Nav has one Google project for all its parts: the same sign-in serves the Android app's plans sync, this web planner and the CVFR Nav Video program's YouTube upload (below), and Google's consent screen shows the name CVFR Nav. The web planner asks Google, in plain words, for: which Google account you are (openid); your account's email address (email), shown in the page's toolbar so you know which account is in, never stored; and to see, create and change only CVFR Nav's own files in a hidden app-data folder of your Google Drive (drive.appdata). It has no access to any other file in your Drive — it can neither see nor touch them — nor to anything else in your Google account. In that folder: the planning files the CVFR Nav Android app keeps there (the same contents as above), the page's own planning file, and the key grant (below). Google's permission (an access token) is held in the page's memory only, renewed with Google while the page is open, never written to the browser's storage; Sign out gives it back to Google. You can also remove CVFR Nav's access on your Google account's third-party access page.
  • Nothing kept by the developer from either sign-in: the developer's services receive no name, email address, Apple or Google account identifier, and no sign-in token.
  • The key, only from your own storage: the planner's program and data are published encrypted. The key to open them is given only to the app: the CVFR Nav app on your iPhone, iPad or Android device asks the developer's service for it (a request proved by App Attest or Play Integrity, as the service's other requests, carrying nothing about you) and saves it as a small record, the key grant — the key's identifier, the key and when it was saved — in your own iCloud private database (iPhone and iPad, with iCloud sync on) or your Drive app-data folder (Android, with Drive sync on); it keeps the current key and the next, deleting older ones. The page reads the grant there after you sign in and holds the key in the page's memory only, never in the browser's storage. Without a grant the page asks you to open the app once. The service limits how often each app may ask and keeps nothing about you; the key is never logged.
  • What stays in the browser: while you are signed in, the page keeps in the browser's local storage on this computer, so you can go on working between visits: a random identifier for this browser as one of your devices, everything your devices sync for planning (plans, pilot details, aircraft records and types, the planning settings), the navigation map's page edits, and the sync's own marks (which of your devices' files it has already read, by checksum). Signing out deletes all of it and reloads the page. On a shared computer, sign out when you are done. A flight file you open in Flights (for the approach debrief) is read on your computer and kept in the page's memory for that visit only — never uploaded or stored. The filing and prior permission emails are handed to your computer's default mail app; the page sends nothing itself. The page sets no cookies of its own and runs no advertising. It has no "keep me signed in" option of its own: only Apple's sign-in session, above, lasts from one visit to the next.
  • Usage statistics, anonymous per visit: to improve the page, it sends the developer's service (on Vercel) a short record of how the page was used during one visit: which screens were open, and events from a fixed list — the sign-in screen shown, a sign-in done (with Apple or Google) or failed, signing out, the builder, Flights or a flight's debrief opened (with its number of approaches), a route typed (its number of points), a point tapped, a Direct leg taken, route alternatives viewed (how many), a plan saved (its number of legs), the plan page or one of its sections opened, the navigation map opened and its options, a box moved or hidden, an ident or a text box added, a print (the paper size and the number of sheets), a PNG, GPX or ForeFlight file saved, the filing email handed to the mail app, a sync done (how long it took) or failed (an error code), an error (its code and where) — each with the time since the visit began; and the page's version and the window's size class (phone, tablet or desktop). It never holds your plans, places or idents typed, positions, name, email, account or any token. The visit is named by a random number made when the page loads and kept in its memory only — never in the browser's storage, never a cookie — so two visits cannot be linked to each other or to you. The service checks the record against the same fixed list and keeps it as one record per visit, with the day (UTC), in the CVFR Nav container's CloudKit database (Apple), readable only by the developer; CloudKit adds the time the record was written. The service never stores your IP address or your browser's user agent; Vercel keeps its short-lived request logs (IP address, time, path) as for every request to the service (see CVFR Nav's weather service above). The records are deleted after 12 months. Nothing is sent when your browser sends Do Not Track or Global Privacy Control.

Test builds (TestFlight)

Pre-release builds given to invited test pilots send recorded flights to the developer while Send recordings to the developer is on (Settings → Test flights) — on by default in those builds, and the tester can switch it off there at any time: the track, the sensor log, the settings the flight was recorded with, the plan flown (route, callsign, aircraft, persons on board — not the filed plan's text nor the weight-and-balance load), the device-health lines (processor use, battery, how warm, Low Power Mode), the NOTAMs and radio reports seen during the flight, crash reports, the name the tester enters, the flight's signature with this device's public signing key (the same on every flight from the device, so it identifies the device; the developer's check notes a signing key not seen before for that tester), and the cockpit audio only if its own switch (off by default) is on too. Test builds also turn a Lightspeed Delta Zulu headset's Flight Recorder on and off at Record and Stop over the headset's own data channel, and write what was exchanged into the flight (the commands and the headset's replies — for a reply that may carry the headset's serial number, only its command and length). The App Store version sends no flights to the developer.

The recordings are stored in Apple's CloudKit (the CVFR Nav container's public database, readable only by the developer), used only to find and fix problems in the app — the developer's legitimate interest in fixing it — and kept up to 12 months, then deleted; sooner on request (cvfr.support@idra-tech.io). Switching Send recordings to the developer off stops any further sending.

CVFR Nav Video (the Windows program)

CVFR Nav Video is a Windows program that makes a debrief video of a light-aircraft flight: the pilot's GoPro footage with the CVFR Nav app's map, the cockpit audio and, if the pilot wants, the app's approach debrief. It is made by the developer of the CVFR Nav app.

What the developer receives: nothing. The program has no account with the developer, no analytics, no advertising and no tracking. It sends the developer no data about you, your flights or your videos.

What stays on your computer:

  • Your flights, GoPro files and videos are read from and written to your own computer and your own iCloud Drive (through Apple's iCloud for Windows). The program makes the video on your computer. Nothing is uploaded unless you turn on the YouTube upload (below).
  • Your choices — the logo, the opening text, where the map sits, and each video's settings — are kept in your Windows user profile.

YouTube upload (optional). The program can upload a finished video to your own YouTube channel. It does so only after you sign in with your Google account and allow it, and only when you have turned the upload on. The sign-in is CVFR Nav's one Google project (see Sign in with Google under The web planner); the program asks it only for what is listed here.

CVFR Nav Video uses YouTube API Services. By uploading through it you agree to the YouTube Terms of Service. Google's handling of your data is described in the Google Privacy Policy.

  • What the program asks Google for: permission to upload videos to your YouTube channel (youtube.upload); permission to manage your YouTube account (youtube), which the program uses only to list your own playlists, make a playlist when you ask it to, and add the video it has just uploaded to the playlist you chose (YouTube has no narrower permission for playlists); and your Google account's email address, to show you which account is signed in. The program never changes or deletes your existing videos or playlists, never reads your other YouTube data (such as comments, subscriptions or history), and cannot see anything else in your Google account.
  • What it sends to YouTube: the video you chose to make, its title, its description (the video's chapters and a line naming the program) and the privacy setting you chose — Private, Unlisted or Public (with a playlist chosen, the playlist's own); a new playlist's name and privacy when you make one; and which playlist the video goes into. Nothing else.
  • What it reads from YouTube: the names, links and privacy of your own playlists, to offer them to you. They are shown in the program and not kept.
  • What it keeps: the permission Google gives back (a "refresh token") and your email address, on your computer only, encrypted with Windows' own data protection so that only your Windows user can read them. They are never sent to the developer or anyone else.
  • How to withdraw it: press Sign out in the program (More options and details → YouTube): the program deletes what it kept and gives the permission back to Google. You can also remove CVFR Nav's access at any time on your Google account's third-party access page. Uninstalling the program deletes what it kept.

Services the program contacts:

  • Your iCloud Drive, through Apple's iCloud for Windows, to read your flights.
  • The developer's support site on GitHub Pages (cvfr.idra-tech.io/version.json), at most once a day, to say when a newer version is out. GitHub keeps its usual request logs; nothing about you is sent.
  • Google and YouTube, only when you sign in or upload, as described above.

The program is meant for pilots and flight instructors. It is not directed at children.
